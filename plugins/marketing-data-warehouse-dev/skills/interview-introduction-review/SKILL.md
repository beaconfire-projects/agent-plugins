# Interview introduction review

Review what the trainee actually says in a practice recording. Produce a report the trainee can use to revise and record again. The audio recording is the only required input. This skill does not require the warehouse MCP server.

## Input and transcription

- Accept only an attached audio recording in a format the current environment can decode. Suggest `.m4a` for phone recordings; `.mp3`, `.wav`, and other decodable audio formats are also fine. One recording may include both the self-introduction and one or more project stories. If there is no recording, ask only for the recording. Do not request an interview round, target role, JD, duration target, resume, script, or other document; do not suggest Word, PDF, or text files as substitutes.
- Use an available audio transcription capability to listen to the recording. Preserve the spoken language and English technical terms in a near-verbatim transcript; mark uncertain words as `[unclear]` and add timestamps or segment markers when the tool provides them. Never fill gaps from an expected script. If the audio cannot be decoded or no transcription capability is available, explain the obstacle and request a compatible recording or audio-capable environment; do not switch to a text or document review.
- Keep the raw transcript separate from a polished sample rewrite. Do not silently clean up grammar in the transcript. Avoid publishing, sharing, or sending the recording or report unless the requester separately asks for it.

## Evaluation

Use brief quotations and timestamps when available to ground each important finding. Distinguish **heard in the recording**, **missing from the recording**, and **unclear**. Missing from a recording does not prove the trainee lacks the experience. Do not invent credentials, publications, citation counts, tools, metrics, scope, or project outcomes.

### Self-introduction

- Does the opening quickly say who the trainee is, what they can do, and a memorable strength supported by the recording? If they mention distinguishing experience, such as a machine-learning PhD or domain project work, check whether they explain its value rather than merely listing it.
- Does the introduction move naturally from background to relevant work and a reason the interviewer should keep listening? Identify generic or robotic claims and replace them with specific, supportable evidence. Evaluate enthusiasm from the words and delivery only when audible; do not infer motivation from accent or speaking style.
- **Interview hook:** Check whether the trainee gives the client a concrete reason to ask the next question, such as an intriguing challenge, decision, tradeoff, or outcome from their own work. Judge whether the hook is specific, credible, easy to follow, and placed early enough to hold interest. Name the likely follow-up question it invites. If absent or weak, suggest one or two natural spoken lines built only from facts in the recording; where a crucial fact is missing, describe what the trainee should confirm before using the line. Do not use clickbait, exaggeration, a bare technology list, or a teaser that hides the basic project summary.
- Recommend important terms only when the recording supports the trainee's use of that skill. A keyword list without explanation is not a good introduction.

### Each project story

- Check whether the first one or two sentences give a concise project summary and the trainee's own role before chronology or implementation detail.
- Check for a clear **business problem → personal actions/solution → business impact** sequence. Note unclear context, missing decisions and tradeoffs, unsupported results, excessive detail, and places where an interviewer may lose the thread.
- Identify relevant technologies or concepts evidenced by the recording but buried in the story. Show how to connect each one to the trainee's action and the problem it solved. Never insert attractive terms, including BigQuery, Cloud Armor, ADK, or Vertex AI, merely because they appear in an example.
- Check ownership language in context. Use “I” for the trainee's actual decisions, implementations, and results; “we” is appropriate for real team work. Flag repeated “we” only where it hides the trainee's specific contribution. Do not rewrite team achievements as solo work.

### Spoken expression

- Note concrete issues affecting comprehension: long lead-ins, chronology before the point, filler repetition, unexplained jargon, vague pronouns, abrupt transitions, or language/grammar problems. If the audio supports it, discuss pace, pauses, and intelligibility with timestamps. Do not score accent or treat code-switching as an error by itself.
- Prioritize the few changes most likely to improve the next interview. Offer natural spoken wording in the language used for the interview practice, not a dense written essay. Keep all sample wording faithful to the recording; use `[confirm metric]` or a question where facts are missing.

## Report

Write in the requester's language unless they ask otherwise; keep the suggested spoken script in the recording's interview language. Include:

1. **Scope and evidence:** recording name/duration if known, language, and transcription limitations.
2. **Top priorities:** 3–5 actionable findings, ordered by interview impact. For each, give the observed excerpt or timestamp, why it weakens the story, and a specific revision.
3. **Self-introduction and hook review:** strengths, missing or unclear differentiators, whether a hook is present, how well it invites a follow-up question, and a short revised opening grounded in the recording. State the likely question the revised hook invites and any fact that needs confirmation.
4. **Project review:** for each project, a compact check of summary, problem, personal solution, impact, relevant technology, and “I/we” clarity. Give a short proposed story outline or sample opening; mark facts the trainee must confirm.
5. **Spoken expression and practice:** only issues supported by the audio, then a short re-recording checklist.
6. **Near-verbatim transcript:** include the full transcript when feasible, with uncertainty markers and timestamps when available. For a long recording, attach or save the full transcript if possible and give a link/path plus a concise excerpt in the report; never omit it without saying why.

Use “not assessable” for sections the recording cannot support. Avoid numerical readiness scores or pass/fail predictions unless the requester supplies a rubric. Do not send the report to the trainee automatically; generate it for review and delivery by the requester.
