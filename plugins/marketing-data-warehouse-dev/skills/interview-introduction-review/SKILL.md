# Interview introduction review

Review what the trainee actually says in a practice recording. Produce a report the trainee can use to revise and record again. The recording is the primary evidence; a resume, JD, project brief, or target-client notes are optional context. This skill does not require the warehouse MCP server.

## Input and transcription

- Accept an attached audio file in a format the current environment can decode. Suggest `.m4a` first because both iOS and Android can export it; `.mp3` and `.wav` are also practical. Do not reject another decodable format solely for its extension. One recording may include both the self-introduction and one or more project stories. Ask for the file if none was supplied.
- If available, request the target role/JD, intended interview language, and verified resume or project facts. Continue with the recording alone when these are absent, and state that role fit and factual accuracy could not be checked against external materials.
- Use an available audio transcription capability to listen to the recording. Preserve the spoken language and English technical terms in a near-verbatim transcript; mark uncertain words as `[unclear]` and add timestamps or segment markers when the tool provides them. Never fill gaps from a resume or an expected script. If audio cannot be decoded or no transcription capability is available, explain the obstacle and request a compatible recording or transcript. A supplied transcript supports a text-only review, clearly labelled as such; do not claim to have assessed pace, pronunciation, or delivery.
- Keep the raw transcript separate from a polished sample rewrite. Do not silently clean up grammar in the transcript. Avoid publishing, sharing, or sending the recording or report unless the requester separately asks for it.

## Evaluation

Use brief quotations and timestamps when available to ground each important finding. Distinguish **heard in the recording**, **missing from the recording**, **unclear**, and **verified from optional materials**. Missing from a recording does not prove the trainee lacks the experience. Do not invent credentials, publications, citation counts, tools, metrics, scope, or project outcomes.

### Self-introduction

- Does the opening say who the trainee is, what role they fit, and a memorable, job-relevant strength? If the trainee has verified distinguishing experience, such as a machine-learning PhD or domain project work, check whether they explain why it matters for this role rather than merely listing it.
- Does the introduction move naturally from background to relevant work and a reason the interviewer should keep listening? Identify generic or robotic claims and replace them with specific, supportable evidence. Evaluate enthusiasm from the words and delivery only when audible; do not infer motivation from accent or speaking style.
- Compare the wording with a supplied JD or client priorities when available. Recommend important terms only when the trainee truly used the related skill and it is relevant. A keyword list without explanation is not a good introduction.

### Each project story

- Check whether the first one or two sentences give a concise project summary and the trainee's own role before chronology or implementation detail.
- Check for a clear **business problem → personal actions/solution → business impact** sequence. Note unclear context, missing decisions and tradeoffs, unsupported results, excessive detail, and places where an interviewer may lose the thread.
- Identify relevant technologies or concepts that are evidenced by the recording or optional verified materials but omitted or buried. Show how to connect each one to the trainee's action and the problem it solved. Never insert attractive terms, including BigQuery, Cloud Armor, ADK, or Vertex AI, merely because they appear in an example or JD.
- Check ownership language in context. Use “I” for the trainee's actual decisions, implementations, and results; “we” is appropriate for real team work. Flag repeated “we” only where it hides the trainee's specific contribution. Do not rewrite team achievements as solo work.

### Spoken expression

- Note concrete issues affecting comprehension: long lead-ins, chronology before the point, filler repetition, unexplained jargon, vague pronouns, abrupt transitions, or language/grammar problems. If the audio supports it, discuss pace, pauses, and intelligibility with timestamps. Do not score accent or treat code-switching as an error by itself.
- Prioritize the few changes most likely to improve the next interview. Offer natural spoken wording in the intended interview language, not a dense written essay. Keep all sample wording faithful to confirmed facts; use `[confirm metric]` or a question where facts are missing.

## Report

Write in the requester's language unless they ask otherwise; keep the suggested spoken script in the intended interview language. Include:

1. **Scope and evidence:** recording name/duration if known, language, any JD or verified background used, and transcription limitations.
2. **Top priorities:** 3–5 actionable findings, ordered by interview impact. For each, give the observed excerpt or timestamp, why it weakens the story, and a specific revision.
3. **Self-introduction review:** strengths, missing or unclear differentiators, relevance to the role, and a short sample opening grounded in verified facts.
4. **Project review:** for each project, a compact check of summary, problem, personal solution, impact, relevant technology, and “I/we” clarity. Give a short proposed story outline or sample opening; mark facts the trainee must confirm.
5. **Spoken expression and practice:** only issues supported by the audio/text, then a short re-recording checklist. If audio was unavailable, mark delivery as not assessed.
6. **Near-verbatim transcript:** include the full transcript when feasible, with uncertainty markers and timestamps when available. For a long recording, attach or save the full transcript if possible and give a link/path plus a concise excerpt in the report; never omit it without saying why.

Use “not assessable” for sections the input cannot support. Avoid numerical readiness scores or pass/fail predictions unless the requester supplies a rubric. Do not send the report to the trainee automatically; generate it for review and delivery by the requester.
